# Idea interview

The output is **fragments**: short, unordered pieces in the user's own words. A fragment is a claim, a story, an example, a number, an objection or a question. Collecting them is the whole job. Structure and polish come later, in another sitting.

## Gather
- The topic. That is enough to begin: what the piece is for and who will read it are interview questions like any other.
- Notes, links or earlier drafts the user already has. Read them first so the interview covers new ground.
- `creator-context.md`, if one is in the working folder, project or attachments: it sets voice, audience, platforms, brand and tools.

## Steps
1. Ask one question per turn, the first turn included, and wait for the answer. Open with the question most likely to get the user talking, such as the moment that made them want to make this.
2. Record each answer as one or more fragments in the user's words, tagged by kind. Keep their phrasing, including the rough parts.
3. Choose the next question from the last answer. Follow whatever they said with the most energy, and ask for the concrete version of anything abstract: the time it happened, the example, the number.
4. Work through these angles over the interview, in whatever order the answers lead:
   - Why this, why now?
   - Who is it for, and what do they believe today?
   - What should they do or think differently afterwards?
   - "Tell me about a time when..."
   - What do most people get wrong about it?
   - Who would disagree, and what would they say?
   - What did you believe before you knew this?
5. Stop when three questions in a row add no new fragment, or when the user says stop.
6. Save the fragments as a file and close with the count, the three strongest fragments, and the gaps: claims with no example, stories with no point.

## Shape
```
# Fragments: <topic>
<date>, <n> fragments

[claim] ...
[story] ...
[example] ...
[number] ...
[objection] ...
[question] ...

Strongest: ...
Gaps: ...
```

## Done when
- Every turn asked exactly one question.
- Every fragment is in the user's words. Nothing was added from general knowledge.
- Every claim has an example or story beside it, or appears under `Gaps`.
- The fragments are unordered: no outline, no headings by section, no draft paragraphs.
- The file is saved and the user knows the next move: shape it into a draft, or build it beat by beat.
