# Proposal writer

**Scope** is the heart of a proposal: what is delivered, what is assumed, what is excluded. A proposal that is clear on those three prevents the dispute that comes later.

## Gather
- Discovery: call notes, emails, the client's brief. Their words for their problem.
- The solution, deliverables and timeline the user intends.
- Pricing, payment terms and validity period, from the user. These are never invented.
- The organization's template and standard terms, if they exist.
- `business-context.md`, if one is in the working folder, project or attachments: it sets voice, brand, products, customers and competitors.

Ask once, in one message, for missing pricing or deliverables. Carry anything still missing as a placeholder.

## Steps
1. `Your needs`: the client's situation and goals, in their language, so they recognize themselves.
2. `Our approach`: how the solution meets each need.
3. `Scope`: deliverables as a numbered list. For a statement of work, give each deliverable an acceptance criterion.
4. `Assumptions`: what the price and timeline depend on.
5. `Out of scope`: the nearby things a client might expect and will not get.
6. `Your responsibilities`: access, information, decisions and turnaround times needed from the client.
7. `Timeline`: milestones with dates or weeks from start.
8. `Investment`: a price table, payment schedule, expenses, validity date. Check the arithmetic in code.
9. `Why us`: two or three proof points relevant to this client.
10. `Next steps`: how to accept, and what happens the day after.
11. For a statement of work, add change control: how changes are requested, priced and approved.
12. Mark legal and commercial terms `[review]` for whoever owns them.

## Done when
- Every need from discovery maps to a deliverable, or is listed out of scope.
- Assumptions and out-of-scope sections are both present.
- Price lines add up to the total.
- Prices, dates and terms come from the user or show as placeholders.
