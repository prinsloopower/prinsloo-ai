# Fiction and memoir

Narrative writing fails differently from expository writing. The vocabulary lists in `ai-tells.md` still apply, but the main problems are in what the narration chooses to state and how scenes are built. Fiction and memoir share most of those patterns and differ sharply in what you may do about them.

## 1. Patterns shared by fiction and memoir

**Emotions stated outright.** "She felt a complex mixture of anger, longing, and hope." The narration names the feeling, often three at once, where an action or a line of dialogue would have carried it. Often the action is already there and the label repeats it.

**Stock physical reactions.** A heart pounding, a breath someone didn't know they were holding, a voice barely above a whisper, eyes that sparkle or darken, a shiver down the spine, a jaw that tightens. Each is a placeholder for what this person would do.

**Generic sensory detail.** Golden light, the smell of coffee or rain or old paper, a gentle breeze, steam curling from a cup. Details that are true of any room of that kind and so tell the reader nothing about this one. They often come in threes.

**Decorative figures.** A simile on most sentences. Abstract nouns paired with concrete ones ("the scent of pine and possibility"). Things that "dance", "whisper" or "weave". The figurative use of tapestry, symphony, kaleidoscope.

**Filtering.** "She noticed", "he realized", "I could see that", "she felt the cold" where the narration could give the thing directly.

**Dialogue that explains.** Characters state their feelings and motives in full sentences, speak in the vocabulary of therapy, summarize the plot to each other, or agree too quickly. Tags carry adverbs ("she said softly") or strain to avoid "said".

**Scenes that close on an aphorism.** "In that moment, she realized that some doors, once opened, can never be closed." The last line turns the scene into a lesson.

**Tidy arcs.** Conflict resolves through a conversation in which everyone understands each other. Endings arrive at reconciliation, acceptance or a quiet epiphany regardless of what came before.

**Announced significance.** "Little did she know", "everything was about to change", "nothing would ever be the same".

**Default names and furniture.** A small set of names that models reach for, with a small set of settings: the old bookshop, the lighthouse, the small coastal town.

**Summary where a scene should be.** Events are reported in general terms ("they talked for hours about everything") at the moments that matter most.

## 2. Fiction: what you may change

The story is the author's. Events, characters, names, relationships, point of view, tense, setting and the substance of what is said in dialogue all stay as they are.

Take the genre, point of view and tense from the text. When the user says the piece is fiction, ask one follow-up if they haven't said: may you invent small details, or should you work only with what is on the page? If that goes unanswered, assume you may not, and start the rewrite without waiting.

In a full rewrite you may:

- Cut stated emotions where an action or line already carries them.
- Cut stock reactions, decorative figures and closing aphorisms.
- Remove filtering so the narration gives the thing directly.
- Tighten dialogue: remove lines that explain what the scene already shows, simplify tags to "said" or nothing, drop the adverbs.
- Rebuild sentences and paragraphing for pace.

You may not, without permission:

- Add events, objects that matter, backstory, character traits or new lines of dialogue.
- Change what a character wants or how a scene ends.
- Rename anyone.

When a passage is generic because the specific detail was never written, cut the generic detail or keep it plain, and offer two or three concrete alternatives in the notes for the author to choose from. Invention is the medium of fiction, and it is the author's to do. If the user says you may invent, write the detail into the text and list each invention in the notes.

Problems above the sentence level, such as a tidy arc or a scene that should be dramatized, are for the notes. Don't restructure the story.

The rewrite will often be much shorter than the original. That is expected. Say so, and say what was cut.

## 3. Fiction example

Original:

> Elara stepped into the old bookshop, the scent of aged paper and forgotten stories washing over her. Her heart pounded in her chest as she saw him. Marcus stood by the window, golden light dancing across his features. "I didn't think you'd come," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. She let out a breath she didn't know she was holding. "I almost didn't," she admitted, feeling a complex mixture of anger, longing, and hope. In that moment, she realized that some doors, once opened, can never truly be closed.

Rewrite:

> Elara went into the old bookshop. Marcus was standing by the window.
>
> "I didn't think you'd come," he said.
>
> "I almost didn't."

Notes:

- Cut: the stock reactions (pounding heart, held breath, whisper), the generic detail (scent of aged paper, golden light), the three named emotions, and the closing line. The two lines of dialogue already carry the tension.
- For you to choose: one thing she notices in this shop that no other shop would have. Something she does on seeing him, in place of the heartbeat. Suggestions on request.
- "Elara" and "the old bookshop" are choices models reach for often. Keep them if they are yours.

## 4. Memoir: what you may change

Memoir and personal essay are nonfiction. Every event, person, place, date, line of remembered dialogue and reported feeling is a claim about what happened in the author's life. Adding a sensory detail to a real memory is fabrication in the same way a made-up statistic is, and the author will be the one answering for it to the people who were there.

So the limits are tighter than in fiction:

- Add nothing. No detail, no dialogue, no feeling, no reflection.
- Don't change how real people are portrayed, including by cutting something that qualified a portrayal.
- Don't alter remembered dialogue beyond removing tags and adverbs.
- Keep chronology and the facts of each event.

Within those limits, a full rewrite may cut and rebuild freely:

- Cut generic sensory detail and decorative figures.
- Cut stock reactions and stated emotions where the event carries them.
- Cut reflections that could be attached to anyone's life: "Looking back, I realize", "it taught me that", "little did I know", lessons listed in threes, appeals to what "we all" feel.
- Cut therapeutic and self-help vocabulary the author doesn't otherwise use: "healing journey", "my truth", "hold space".
- State what happened in plain sentences.

Reflection belongs in memoir, so cutting it needs care. A reflection that is particular to this author, in words that sound like them, stays. A generic one goes, and every cut reflection is listed in the notes so the author can restore what was theirs. When unsure which kind it is, keep it and ask.

The result is usually a shorter, plainer draft with gaps. That is the correct output. The gaps are where the real material goes, and only the author has it.

## 5. Memoir: questions for the author

The questions matter as much as the rewrite. For each place where you cut something generic, ask for the real thing it stood in for. Good questions are concrete and answerable from memory:

- What was in the room, on the table, out the window?
- What did they say, as near as you remember it? What did you say back?
- What did you do next?
- What do you remember that doesn't fit the story as you've told it?
- The draft says this changed you. What happened later that showed it?
- How old were you? What time of year was it? Who else was there?

Ask about the three or four places where a real detail would do the most, not every sentence. Put the questions in the notes, tied to the passage they refer to. Use an `[AUTHOR: ...]` marker in the text only where a sentence can't stand without the answer.

## 6. Things to leave alone in both

- **The narrator's voice.** An ornate, plain, comic or fragmentary style may be deliberate. Look at the passages that are clearly the author's and follow them. If the whole piece is ornate by choice, say what you see and ask before flattening it.
- **How characters speak.** People talk in clichés, repeat themselves and use bad grammar. That is characterization. Keep dialect and idiom.
- **Regional and idiosyncratic phrasing in memoir.** Turns of phrase a style checker would flag are often the most valuable sentences in the piece.
- **Deliberate repetition and rhythm.** A refrain is not synonym-avoidance failing.
- **Genre conventions.** Romance, thriller, fantasy and literary fiction each have expected moves. A convention used on purpose is not a pattern to remove.
- **Dashes for interrupted speech.**
