# Autopilot patterns

A catalog of the patterns readers take as machine-written, grouped by family. Each family says what the pattern looks like, what it is usually standing in for, what to do about it, and when to leave it alone.

## 1. How to use this catalog

Use it to notice, then go back to the sentence and ask what it was for. The catalog has no replacement column on purpose. Swapping a flagged word for a neighbor leaves the sentence as empty as before, and the neighbor becomes the next pattern.

Three cautions apply to every entry:

- **One occurrence is nothing.** These are ordinary English. The signal is a cluster, or the same construction repeating through a piece.
- **Genre conventions are not patterns.** Headings in a report, hedges in a paper and passive voice in a methods section belong there. Check the defaults for the type before flagging.
- **Look for the ordinary explanation.** Three items may be three real things. A literal or technical use of a flagged word is fine: leverage in finance, ecosystem in ecology, landscape in geography, robust in statistics, navigate in an interface.

No count of these patterns shows who wrote a text. People write all of them.

## 2. Vocabulary

**Looks like.** Words that sound substantial and commit to nothing.

- Inflated verbs: delve, leverage, harness, navigate (figurative), foster, bolster, underscore, showcase, garner, streamline, revolutionize, empower, unlock, elevate, embark, resonate, exemplify.
- Abstract nouns used figuratively: landscape, realm, tapestry, journey, ecosystem, synergy, paradigm shift, game-changer, testament, interplay, intersection.
- Stock adjectives: robust, seamless, cutting-edge, innovative, comprehensive, pivotal, crucial, nuanced, compelling, transformative, multifaceted, intricate, overarching, unprecedented, vibrant, profound, renowned, groundbreaking, enduring, myriad, plethora.

**Standing in for.** The specific thing the writer didn't state. "Leverages cutting-edge technology" stands in for what the product does and how.

**Do.** State the literal thing if the source has it. Otherwise delete the word, and often the sentence.

**Leave alone.** Literal and technical uses, single occurrences, and a word that is the author's habit in their own samples.

## 3. Openers and closers

**Looks like.**

- Chat residue: "Great question!", "Certainly!", "Here is the essay you asked for", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if you'd like me to expand".
- Scene-setting openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "In an era of", "In the ever-evolving world of".
- Openers that restate the title or announce the plan: "In this article, we will explore".
- Closers that summarize what was just said: "In conclusion", "Overall", "Ultimately".
- Closers that gesture at the future: "The future looks bright", "Only time will tell", "Exciting times lie ahead".
- Closers that fish for a response: "What do you think? Share your thoughts below."
- Knowledge-gap disclaimers: "While specific details are limited", "Based on available information".

**Standing in for.** A beginning and an ending. The writer didn't know where the piece starts or didn't trust it to end.

**Do.** Delete chat residue. Start where the content starts and stop where it stops. For a knowledge-gap disclaimer, cut the claim it hedges or mark it for the author.

**Leave alone.** "As of" with a date on time-sensitive data. A summary section in a long report whose readers expect one. A plain call to action the author wants.

## 4. Filler and signposting

**Looks like.**

- Announcing instead of saying: "It's worth noting that", "It's important to remember", "It bears mentioning", "Let's dive in", "Let's break this down", "Here's what you need to know".
- Throat-clearing: "At its core", "At the end of the day", "When it comes to", "The reality is", "The truth is", "In terms of".
- Long forms of short words: "in order to", "due to the fact that", "at this point in time", "has the ability to", "in the event that".

**Standing in for.** Emphasis. The writer wants the next clause to feel important without making it so.

**Do.** Delete the announcement and keep the clause. Use the short word.

**Leave alone.** A signpost in a long technical document that tells the reader where they are.

## 5. Inflated significance and promotional tone

**Looks like.**

- Importance asserted without content: "marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament to", "plays a crucial role", "reflects broader trends", "sets the stage for", "has garnered significant attention", "leaves an indelible mark".
- Brochure language: "boasts", "nestled in the heart of", "breathtaking", "must-visit", "rich heritage", "commitment to excellence", "world-class".

**Standing in for.** The fact that would show why the thing matters.

**Do.** State the fact if the source has it. If the source has only the assertion, cut it. Don't replace it with a different assertion of importance.

**Leave alone.** A significance claim the author argues for and supports.

## 6. Vague attribution

**Looks like.** "Experts agree", "studies show", "industry reports suggest", "observers have noted", "many believe", "it is widely recognized".

**Standing in for.** A source.

**Do.** Name the source if it is in the material or the user provides it. Otherwise cut the claim, or keep it as the author's own statement if they are in a position to make it, or mark it `[AUTHOR: source needed]`. Never supply a source yourself.

**Leave alone.** A general attribution followed by the citation.

## 7. Hedges, intensifiers and sincerity markers

**Looks like.**

- Stacked hedges: "could potentially", "might possibly suggest", "it could be argued that", "to some extent".
- Concession reflexes: "While there are certainly challenges", "This is not without its drawbacks", "To be sure", "That said".
- Intensifiers: "truly", "really", "incredibly", "extremely", "absolutely", "deeply".
- Sincerity markers: "honestly", "frankly", "genuinely", "to be clear", "I'll be honest", "let me be direct".

**Standing in for.** Stacked hedges avoid committing to a claim. Intensifiers and sincerity markers try to add force or candor from outside the sentence, and they imply the surrounding text is something less.

**Do.** Keep one hedge where the doubt applies and say what it depends on. Delete intensifiers and sincerity markers.

**Leave alone.** Any hedge that reflects real uncertainty, at the strength the author gave it. In academic writing, treat every hedge as meaningful until shown otherwise.

## 8. Sentence constructions

**Looks like.**

- Denying a framing nobody raised: "It's not just X, it's Y", "This isn't about X. It's about Y.", "Not only X but also Y", "No X. No Y. Just Z."
- Reflexive triples: three adjectives or three phrases where the third adds nothing ("innovation, inspiration, and insight").
- Avoiding "is" and "has": "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "represents", "boasts", "features", used throughout a piece.
- Trailing participle phrases that add commentary: "highlighting the importance of", "underscoring the need for", "reflecting a broader trend", "contributing to".
- False ranges: "from X to Y" where X and Y are not ends of any scale.
- Synonym cycling: one thing called by four names in a paragraph to avoid repeating a word.
- Reveals: a colon or question set up for a punchline. "The result: chaos." "The catch? It's expensive."
- Rhetorical questions answered at once: "So what does this mean? It means".
- A quotable line ending each paragraph, restating the paragraph as an aphorism.
- Runs of sentences with the same opening word or the same shape.
- Stock framings: "Imagine a world where", "Picture this", "Think of it as", "X meets Y".

**Standing in for.** Rhythm and emphasis in place of a claim. Most of these make a sentence sound finished.

**Do.** State the positive claim. Use the number of items there are. Use "is" and "has". Turn a trailing participle into its own sentence if it carries a real claim, and delete it if it doesn't. Pick one term and repeat it. Cut the paragraph-ending aphorism; the paragraph already ended.

**Leave alone.** A contrast the author is really drawing, where someone does hold the view being denied. Three items that are three things. A single "serves as" in formal prose.

## 9. Transitions

**Looks like.** "Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally", "In addition", "That being said", "With that in mind", "Moving forward", "On the other hand" with no first hand.

**Standing in for.** A logical connection the writer didn't work out.

**Do.** Use the connection that is really there (because, so, but, then), or none. Often the next sentence can simply start.

**Leave alone.** Formal connectives in academic and legal writing, used where the logic matches.

## 10. Structure

**Looks like.**

- Every section built on one template: setup, explanation, takeaway.
- Paragraphs of near-identical length and shape.
- A "Challenges" or "Future outlook" section that every topic gets.
- "Despite its strengths, X faces challenges. Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive."
- The balanced conclusion: good news, bad news, hopeful close.
- Lists whose items all have the same grammar and length, or bullet fragments where the reasoning should be.
- Headings over sections of one or two sentences.
- One sentence per line, with white space doing the work of argument.
- A summary of what the reader just read.

**Standing in for.** An organization that comes from the content. The template was applied before the content was known.

**Do.** Let each section be as long as its content. Merge takeaways into the text. Turn fragment bullets back into sentences that show how the points connect. Cut the template sections unless the source has real content for them.

**Leave alone.** Consistent structure in reference documents, where predictability helps the reader. Parallel list items that are parallel things.

## 11. Formatting and typography

**Looks like.**

- Bold scattered across phrases that are not more important than their neighbors.
- Lists where each item is a bolded label, a colon and a sentence restating the label.
- Emoji on headings and bullets.
- Em dashes, used often and for dramatic asides.
- Title Case On Every Heading.
- Horizontal rules between short sections; tables for content with one dimension.
- Arrows and symbols in prose.

**Standing in for.** Emphasis and organization the sentences don't provide.

**Do.** Remove decoration. Rewrite a label-and-colon list as a sentence or two. Rebuild sentences so they don't need a dash: use a comma, a period or parentheses. Readers now treat the em dash as a marker, so the default is to remove them. Count before saying how many there were.

**Leave alone.** Em dashes if the author's own samples use them, and dashes for interrupted speech in fiction. Title case when a style guide requires it. Curly quotation marks in formatted prose, which are simply correct typography. Bold for a defined term or a real warning.

## 12. Over-correction

Patterns produced by trying to sound human. Check every rewrite for these, because they are the ones you are most likely to have added.

**Looks like.**

- Short punchy sentences and fragments placed for effect: "That's it." "Full stop." "Not anymore."
- Casual openers: "Look,", "Here's the thing", "Let's be real", "And honestly?".
- Confessional asides: "I keep coming back to", "Here's what gets me", "I'll admit".
- Invented experience or feeling in first person.
- Slang or a chatty register dropped into serious material.
- Planted roughness: typos, sentences that trail off, digressions that go nowhere.
- Telegraphic compression: dropped articles and connectives, undefined abbreviations, symbols in place of sentences.

**Standing in for.** A voice. The writer added the surface features of a person talking and left the content as it was.

**Do.** Remove them unless they were in the author's original or match the author's samples. Plain and direct is enough.

**Leave alone.** The author's own habits, whatever they are.
